# Tower defence as combat

Research date: 2026-11-14. This is a research proposal, not a prototype verdict.

**Recommendation (opinion):** test tower defence as a fight mode on the first-person depth lanes (e16), not as a new view. Lanes are the most readable form of tower defence, the owner already likes lanes, and the two board views (e13, e15) were rejected as messy. Take five ideas from tower defence: a visible wave queue as the enemy intent, a setup turn, summons that act on their own like towers, unguarded lanes that hurt the hero, and pushing enemies back as a way to buy turns. Build each idea as a switch, so bots can show which ones help, and compare against plain e16 on the same seeds.

## Evidence and limits

I read `design/direction.md`, `design/combat-exploration.md`, `research/combat-views.md`, `research/autobattler-combat.md` and the e13 and e14 `RESULT.md` files. The e16 research (`research/depth-lanes.md`) was not published when I wrote this, so I don't know e16's exact rules. Where this report needs a depth-lane rule (reach by depth, cost of a side step, blockers), it gives a default and says to use e16's rule instead once it exists.

Game descriptions below come from developer pages, reviews, wikis and player discussions, linked in place. Wikis and guides can be out of date. No game was played or timed for this report. The lessons, rules and numbers are design opinions and experiments to run, not facts. Where a source didn't confirm something, I say so.

Owner verdicts so far, which this report takes as given: fighting on the exploration grid (e11) is boring, the tactics board (e13) was messy and tedious, side-view lane slots (e14) are promising, and the hex autobattler (e15) was too messy.

Small turn-based deckbuilding tower-defence games exist (for example [Echo Bastion](https://www.vgdb.co/games/echo-bastion) and [Towers Deck](https://store.steampowered.com/app/2261750/Towers_Deck)). ### What the prior art says, in short

- **Lanes beat mazes for reading.** Plants vs. Zombies switched from a maze to lanes. Sanctum players found mazing hard in first person. Defender's Quest dropped mazing on purpose, to "limit the number of choices the player had to make at any given time".
- **Units as towers is proven.** Defender's Quest, Arknights and Kingdom Rush barracks all turn characters into placed blockers or shooters with abilities.
- **Turn-based tower defence works.** Monster Train (one step per round) and Inscryption (a visible back row) are the models for our turn structure.
- **One solution per map is the main failure.** Thronefall and Dungeon of the Endless reviewers name it. Rogue Tower and Isle of Arrows fight it by changing the map.
- **Idle time is the other failure.** The Defender's Quest designer says players "run out of actions". Defend the Rook's reviewer disliked waiting on animations. A Siege of Centauri developer warns that if players act only every 30 seconds, it is "mostly watching and waiting". [Stardock dev journal](https://forums.deadmansdrawgame.com/495008/siege-of-centauri-dev-journal-what-makes-a-good-tower-defense-game)
- **A hero with nothing to do kills it.** Kingdom Two Crowns shows the extreme. Thronefall, Orcs Must Die and Dungeon Defenders give the hero a real fight job.

## 2. What tower defence adds, and what can go wrong

### What it adds that our other formats lack

- **Waves are intents with a time axis.** Today an intent says what happens next turn. A wave queue says what arrives in two and four turns, and in which lane. That gives the player a reason to plan two turns ahead, not just react.
- **A setup turn.** You see the whole fight before it starts and place the wolf and a few things. e13 and e14 start in contact, so the first turn is often a scramble. e14 reports 20+ incoming damage on the hero in round 1.
- **Fewer commands, same decisions.** Placed summons act on their own. e13's result says "Controlling four units adds clicks more than decisions". Towers fix that without making the whole fight automatic, which was e15's problem.
- **Distance is time.** An enemy five rows away is four turns away. Pushing it back two rows buys two turns and drags it through your traps again. Our push and pull get a second meaning beyond damage.
- **Pressure spread over time.** Many weaker enemies arriving in waves, instead of four strong ones at once, could make fights less lethal on turn 1 without making them easy.
- **Fights that are not "kill everything".** Hold the door for 8 turns, stop the thief, guard the fallen ally. Those fight goals come naturally from tower defence and also cap fight length.

## 3. How our pieces map

| Our piece                     | Tower-defence role                               | Notes                                                                                                                                             |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Hero                          | Builder and spell caster who also holds a lane   | Plays all cards. Steps sideways along the party row. Blocks the lane it stands in, like a Kingdom Rush hero.                                      |
| Wolf                          | Mobile blocker, commanded by the player          | Blocks the cell it stands on, three abilities on cooldowns, and Heel to get back to safety. The wolf stays a partner, not a tower.                |
| Summons (max 2)               | Towers                                           | Placed by cards, last the whole fight, act on their own each turn. Bone Servant blocks and shoves. Spirit Totem shoots down its lane.             |
| Cards                         | Build, trap and spell cards                      | Summon cards are the towers. Oil, Frost and Miasma are traps. Strike, Firebolt, Hook and Repel are spells.                                        |
| Ground effects                | Traps on lane cells                              | Fire, oil, ice and poison sit on cells and trigger when enemies enter or end a turn there. Ward sits on party cells.                              |
| Push and pull                 | Moving enemies along the route                   | Back means buying time and going through traps again. Forward means pulling into a kill zone. Sideways means changing lanes.                      |
| Enemy intents                 | Route arrows, attack numbers, and the wave queue | Each enemy shows "moves to row 3" or "hits the servant for 5". The queue shows the next waves per lane with their arrival turn.                   |
| Room from the level generator | The map                                          | Width gives the lane count, length gives the depth, pillars block a cell, and doors pick the entry lanes. A side door adds an entry halfway down. |
| Fight length                  | Waves and goals                                  | 2 to 3 waves. Target 7 to 10 turns.                                                                                                               |

The room mapping is light on purpose. Exact room geometry made e13 crowded, so lanes take only the room's width, length, pillars and doors.

## 4. Three options, one per view

### Option A: Lane siege in first person (e16's view)

**Rules sketch.** The party stands on row 0 of 3 lanes and faces down the room. Rows 1 to 5 are the approach, and row 6 is the dark at the far end where the next wave waits, visible as silhouettes. Enemies step one row per turn down their lane. An enemy can't enter a cell held by one of your units and attacks it instead. Summons and traps go on approach cells. The hero steps sideways along row 0. Hero cards reach by depth: Strike hits row 1, Firebolt the first enemy in rows 1 to 4. An enemy that walks into an empty party cell breaches: it hits the hero for double damage and goes back to the dark in its lane.

**Flow of a fight.** Setup turn: read the wave list, pick the hero's lane, place the wolf, play opening cards with 5 energy. Turns 1 to 3: wave 1 walks in, and you place a servant, oil a cell and Firebolt it when the brute steps on it. Turn 4: wave 2 shows up in the queue, two skitters in the right lane, so you shift the hero right and Frost row 2. Turn 6: wave 3 brings an archer that stops at row 4, so you Hook it into your fire. The fight ends around turn 9.

**Numbers to start from.** 3 lanes, 5 approach rows, 2 to 3 waves of 2 to 4 enemies, waves 3 turns apart, enemies with about half of e14's HP, hero 28 HP.

**What would make it fail.** e16's own core doesn't work (this option rides on it). Blockers hold whole lanes, so one layout wins. Summons do the work and the pass bot wins. The first-person depth is hard to read past row 3.

### Option B: Approach corridor in side view (e14's view)

**Rules sketch.** e14's 4 slots per side, plus 4 approach cells between the two groups and a door on the right. Enemy waves come through the door and walk one cell per turn toward your slot 1. e14's slot rules decide reach: Strike reaches approach cell 1 from slot 1, and Firebolt reaches cells 1 to 4 from slots 2 to 4. Summons and traps go on approach cells. The next wave waits behind the door as icons.

**Flow of a fight.** Setup: place a servant in cell 2 and oil cell 3. Wave 1 piles up behind the servant on the oil, and Firebolt makes a blaze. Wave 2 brings an archer that shoots over the servant, so you swap the hero back and pull the archer forward with the wolf.

**Numbers to start from.** 4 approach cells, e14's unit stats, 2 to 3 waves of 2 to 3 enemies.

**What would make it fail.** One line means few placement choices, about 4 cells, so "blocker in cell 1, fire behind it" probably wins every fight. Enemies stop sitting in fixed slots, which weakens the e14 slot puzzle the owner liked. It is cheap to build from e14's code, but it may only show that one line is too narrow.

### Option C: Room siege in top view (e13's view)

**Rules sketch.** The generated room seen from above. The party holds the doorway it came in through. Enemies enter from the other doors, announced a turn ahead as e13 already does, and walk the shortest four-way route to the party. Their routes are drawn with a tick for each turn. Summons block tiles, routes are recomputed, and the preview shows the new routes. The wolf moves 3 and blocks. The hero stays within 1 tile of the hold point and casts.

**Flow of a fight.** Setup: put a servant in the north door so the route bends past a brazier, and oil the bend. Wave 1 walks the long way and you topple the brazier. Wave 2 enters from the east door, and you move the wolf to block while the totem shoots.

**Numbers to start from.** Rooms 7×7 to 9×9 (e13 found 5 to 7 tiles too small), 1 to 3 entry doors, 3 waves, 8 to 12 enemies.

**What would make it fail.** It repeats e13's mess: crowded rooms, overlays, many intents. Recomputed routes confuse ("why did it turn left?"). Blocking the door with a servant and burning the corridor behind it likely wins every room. It is the closest to two views the owner already rejected.

## 5. Recommendation

**Option A, built as a mode of the depth-lane format, not as a new view.** The reasons:

- Plants vs. Zombies is lane tower defence, and e16 already borrows from it. The open question is how many tower-defence ideas to add, not which view to use.
- Option C brings back the board clutter that sank e13 and e15. Option B's single line gives too few placement choices.
- First person keeps the exploration camera, so fights don't leave the dungeon view at all.
- Some ideas also fit e14 cheaply: a visible reinforcement queue and summons that act on their own. If e16 doesn't work out, try those two in e14 before building Option B.

Build the tower-defence ideas as switches and run them against plain e16 on the same seeds. If the full mode isn't clearly better than plain e16, tower defence is a feature (say, the wave queue and self-acting summons) and not a format.

## 6. Proposed prototype: e17 lane siege

Working name `prototypes/e17-lane-siege`. If e16's rules code exists, reuse it and add a `siege` switch set. If the owner prefers, make it a mode inside e16 instead. Everything below is an experiment. Numbers are first guesses for tuning. Where e16 has settled a rule (reach by depth, side-step cost, blocking), use e16's rule so the comparison is only about the tower-defence ideas.

### Core loop

1. Enter a room in first person. The camera stays put. Lanes light up on the floor in front of the party.
2. **Setup turn.** The full wave list is shown: each wave's enemies, lanes and arrival turn. Pick the hero's lane, place the wolf on any cell in rows 0 to 2, draw 5, and spend 5 energy.
3. **Player phase.** Draw to 5 (discard the rest), get 3 energy (keep up to 1 unspent, so at most 4). The hero plays cards and may step one lane for free (a second step costs 1 energy). The wolf may move one cell and use one ability.
4. **Tower phase.** Summons act on their own, front row first, left lane first.
5. **Enemy phase.** All enemies act at once. If something is in the next cell, they attack it. Otherwise they move along their lane. Ranged enemies stop at their range and shoot. Then ground effects tick.
6. **Wave step.** Enemies due this turn step from the dark (row 6) into row 5. If row 5 is taken, they wait. Intents update.
7. Win when every wave is dead (or when the turn count is reached in a hold fight). Lose when the hero dies. Turn 16 is a loss (anti-stall, as in e13).

### Board

- 3 lanes by 6 rows. Row 0 is the party row, rows 1 to 5 are the approach, and row 6 is the dark (not targetable; shows the queue).
- One unit per cell. A pillar cell can't be entered. An enemy facing a pillar sidesteps into the neighbour lane closer to the middle, and its intent arrow shows it.
- **Breach:** an enemy that moves into an empty row-0 cell hits the hero for twice its damage (ward applies), then goes back to row 6 in the same lane and queues again. The total number of enemies stays fixed, so fights still end.
- Optional side door (generated rooms): enemies can enter at row 3 of an edge lane, shown in the queue like any wave.

### Ground, push and pull

- **Fire:** 3 on entry and at end of turn, lasts 2 turns. **Fire on oil:** blaze, 5 on entry and at end of turn, spreads to connected oil in the same lane.
- **Ice:** an enemy entering stops there for the turn. Fire melts ice (the ice is removed, no extra effect).
- **Poison:** 1 at end of turn, lasts 3 turns, doesn't spread.
- **Ward:** on party cells only, hits deal 2 less.
- **Push and pull:** a moved enemy triggers every hazard cell it crosses, once per cell. Into a unit: both take 1 and the move stops. Pushed sideways into a room wall: 2 damage. Pushed back past row 5: it goes back to the dark and comes in again next turn. No chain collisions.
- e13 found fire on poison too easy, so poison and fire don't combine in this test.

Fight goals (extra, chosen per fight): **Clear** (kill every wave), **Hold** (survive to turn 8; leftover enemies don't matter), **Thief** (a Grave Robber, 6 HP and speed 1, walks to row 0 and escapes with the fight's reward if it gets there; it never attacks).

### Six hand-made fights

1. **Cellar Steps.** Clear. Two waves: brutes and one skitter. Teaches placing a servant and what a breach costs.
2. **Archer Gallery.** Clear. Archers in two lanes behind a brute. Blockers don't help; Hook and fire do.
3. **Oil Cistern.** Clear. Starts with oil in the middle lane, rows 2 and 3, and brutes in the edge lanes. Shove and Hook move them onto the oil.
4. **Skitter Nest.** Clear. Three fast waves in all lanes. Tests Frost, Heel and the setup turn.
5. **Hold the Door.** Hold to turn 8. A steady stream of enemies. Repel and Frost buy time. Killing doesn't win.
6. **Warden's Choir.** Clear, with a thief. A shieldbearer escorts a hexer, an archer guards the far end, and a grave robber runs down the lane you are weakest in.

### Generator

`generate(seed, difficulty)` for difficulty 1 to 6:

- An enemy budget by difficulty. Waves 2 to 3, waves 2 to 4 turns apart.
- Lane choice by seed, with limits: at difficulty 1 and 2, no wave uses all three lanes at once.
- Room features from the e11 level generator: 0 to 1 pillar, a side door 20% of the time, a starting hazard 30% of the time, and a goal (Clear 70%, Hold 20%, Thief 10%).
- **Validator:** every lane can be reached, the first wave can't reach row 0 before turn 3, total threat stays in budget, and the greedy bot can win at difficulty 1. Log rejections and their reasons.

### Five rules most likely to make it fun

1. **Show the whole fight up front.** The wave list and the routes are the intents. A surprise should never be what decides a fight.
2. **Summons are weak alone.** The pass bot must lose almost every fight. The towers set up the play; the hero's cards finish it.
3. **The hero's lane matters every turn.** Attack cards reach only down the hero's lane, so stepping is a real choice and not busywork.
4. **No lane can be held forever.** Brutes break blockers, skitters hop, archers stop short and hexers poison your traps.
5. **Pushing back buys time.** Repel, the servant's shove and sideways Shove are the tower-defence version of our displacement. They should beat raw damage in some fights.

## 7. Bots, metrics and failure gates

Pure rules code drives the game, the tests, the bots and the previews, as in e13 and e14. All bots see the same information: the wave list yes, the future draw order no.

**Bots:** random, greedy (one action at a time by immediate score, counting shown intents), and strong (beam search over the whole turn, scored after the enemy phase, like e14). Simple policies, to look for dominant patterns:

- **pass:** never plays anything after setup. Shows whether the towers play themselves.
- **no setup:** skips the setup turn.
- **fixed layout:** the same setup every fight. Use the best template found on training seeds.
- **turtle:** puts every summon and trap in the hero's lane, and the hero never steps.
- **passive hero:** the hero places summons and traps but never steps or plays an attack card.
- **damage only:** only Strike, Firebolt and Bite.

**Switches** (each on or off, the rest held fixed): wave queue visible, setup turn, summons acting on their own (off means the player commands them, as in e13 and e14), breach rule, banking 1 energy. With all switches off, the mode is close to plain e16.

| Metric                | What to report                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Success and pacing    | Win rate per bot and difficulty with Wilson intervals. Turns per fight (median and p90). Leaks per fight. Hero, wolf and summon survival.                                                                                                              |
| Placement matters     | Same turn policy (strong), setup varied: strong setup vs random legal setups vs fixed layout. Report win and HP delta, and the share of fights whose result changes.                                                                                   |
| One dominant layout   | Best fixed template on held-out seeds vs adaptive setup. Heat map of where the strong bot puts summons and traps. Share of summons on the single most common cell. Setup overlap between fights (Jaccard of occupied cells).                           |
| Pre-planning matters  | Wave queue on vs off, and setup turn on vs off, on the same seeds.                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| Watch burden          | Share of turns where passing is within the near-best margin. Turns with no useful legal play. Estimated enemy-phase animation time from event counts with fixed durations (moves at the same time count once). Share of fight time spent in animation. |
| Hero has things to do | Hero cards' share of damage dealt and damage prevented. Hero steps per fight, and how often the best line includes a step. Win gap of passive hero and turtle vs strong.                                                                               |
| Wolf                  | Death rate (e14 was 71%), blocks per fight, Shove and Heel uses, and the win change when its abilities are disabled but its body stays.                                                                                                                |
| Feature use           | Per fight: summons placed, traps triggered, pushes back, sideways shoves, blazes, enemies forced through hazards, archers pulled. Win change when each is disabled.                                                                                    |
| Decision variety      | Distinct outcomes per turn and near-best options, with e14's definition so numbers compare. Repeat rate of the best turn kind.                                                                                                                         |
| Format vs feature     | All switches on vs plain e16 on the same seeds: win spread between policies, turns, watch burden, hero share.                                                                                                                                          |
| Predictability        | Preview vs result mismatches, replay hash mismatches, generator rejection reasons.                                                                                                                                                                     |
| Performance           | Bot ms per fight, search nodes per turn, browser frame time at the established target.                                                                                                                                                                 |

Use at least 300 generated seeds split by difficulty, plus 30 runs of each hand-made fight. Use paired seeds for every on/off comparison and bootstrap the paired differences.

**Failure gates (first guesses, not standards):**

- **Placement doesn't matter:** random legal setups within 5 points of the strong setup.
- **One dominant layout:** a fixed template within 5 points of adaptive setup on held-out fights, or more than half of all summons on one cell.
- **Pre-planning isn't real:** hiding the wave queue costs less than 5 points.
- **Towers play themselves:** the pass bot wins more than 20%.
- **Watching dominates:** passing is near-best in more than 40% of turns, the median enemy phase is over 3 s, or the median fight is over 12 turns.
- **Hero has little to do:** passive hero within 10 points of strong, or hero cards supply less than 25% of damage dealt plus damage prevented.
- **Wolf is still a speed bump:** it dies in more than 50% of fights, or removing its abilities barely changes results.
- **Decided at setup:** if swapping in a random turn policy after a strong setup barely changes results, the fight ends at setup. That is the opposite failure and also fails the brief.
- **Not worth a format:** if all switches on is no better than plain e16 on policy spread, watch burden and hero share, keep the one or two switches that helped and drop the mode.
- **Broken:** any preview mismatch or replay divergence, whatever the win rate.
